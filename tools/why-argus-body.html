About the name

Why Argus?

Argus Panoptes, "Argus the all-seeing", was the watchman of Greek myth: a giant with eyes all over his body who never stopped keeping guard. That is the job I wanted this tool to do for a Microsoft 365 tenant.

The myth

The watchman who never slept

The goddess Hera needed a guard she could trust. Zeus had turned Io into a white heifer to hide her, and Hera wanted her watched day and night. She chose Argus, the giant whose name became a byword for vigilance. Ovid gives him a hundred eyes; an older poem says simply that sleep never fell upon his eyes. Later writers explained it this way: Argus closed only a few eyes at a time, so some were always open.

Zeus sent Hermes to free Io. Hermes came disguised as a shepherd and talked and played music until, one by one, every eye closed. Then he killed the watchman, and was known ever after as Argeiphontes, the slayer of Argus.

Hera did not let her watchman be forgotten. She set his hundred eyes in the tail of her sacred bird, the peacock, where they still look out today.

Sources: Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book 1; Apollodorus, Library 2.1.3; the Hesiodic Aigimios, fragment 294.

Why it fits

Four ideas from the story, built into the tool

01

Many eyes, each on its own watch

Argus looked every way at once. Argus the tool runs 26 reports, each watching a different corner of the tenant (sign-ins, guests, licences, app secrets, Conditional Access, devices) on its own schedule.

02

Some eyes are always open

The giant never closed all his eyes at once. Scheduled runs keep checking whether or not anyone is looking at the console, and failed runs raise an alert instead of failing quietly.

03

What finally beat him was noise

Hermes did not out-fight the watchman; he talked him to sleep. Alert fatigue does the same to admins. That is why Argus emails only when a rule matches: a threshold, a change, an anomaly or a genuinely new row.

04

The eyes outlived the watch

Hera kept his eyes in the peacock's tail. Argus keeps every run, its rows and its logs, so you can see what changed and when, and automations keep a before and after snapshot of every change they make.

From the author

Why I chose him

Most of the trouble I have seen in Microsoft 365 tenants was never hidden. The failed sign-ins, the guest nobody remembered inviting and the app secret that expired over a weekend were all sitting in Microsoft Graph, waiting for someone to ask. Nobody asked, because asking meant another script, another schedule and another inbox full of reports nobody read.

I wanted a watchman instead: something that keeps looking when I'm not, stays quiet when nothing has changed, and speaks up when something has. Argus Panoptes was the obvious name. His is the oldest story we have about vigilance, and his ending is a reminder of how a watchman fails: not because he can't see, but because he is lulled into not looking.

Rohi Rikman
